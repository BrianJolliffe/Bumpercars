# Relaxing & Settling a Newborn to Bed — A Gentle Step-by-Step Guide (from ~1 week old)

> **Please read first:** This is a plain-language summary of guidance from trusted
> sources (American Academy of Pediatrics / HealthyChildren.org, NHS, Mayo Clinic,
> Cleveland Clinic, Stanford Children's Health, the Sleep Foundation, the Lullaby
> Trust, Safe to Sleep / NICHD, La Leche League, Raising Children Network, Tresillian,
> and Harvey Karp / Happiest Baby). It is **not** medical advice. Newborn guidance
> genuinely varies between countries and experts, and every baby is different — always
> follow your own pediatrician, midwife, or health visitor.

---

## First, set your expectations (this is the most calming step)

A one-week-old is *not* supposed to sleep through the night. Knowing this protects
your own sanity:

- **They sleep a lot, in short bursts.** Roughly **16–17 hours a day** (the NHS gives
  a wide normal range of ~8–18 hrs), but usually only **1–3 hours at a time**.
- **They wake to feed every 2–3 hours.** Their stomachs are tiny; frequent waking,
  day and night, is normal and necessary.
- **They have no body clock yet.** Newborns can't tell day from night — a real
  day/night rhythm doesn't mature until about **8–12 weeks**, and sleeping 6–8 hours
  at night typically isn't until around **3 months**.
- **Their sleep cycles are short (~1 hour)** and start in light, active (REM) sleep,
  so they stir and wake easily.
- **You cannot "spoil" a newborn, and you do not sleep-train this young.** Responding
  promptly to cries actually leads to *less* crying and builds security. The job at one
  week is simply to **feed, comfort, and respond to cues** — not to enforce a schedule.

---

## Step 1 — Watch the baby, not the clock: catch tired cues early

Overtired newborns are much *harder* to settle, so the goal is to start winding down at
the **first** signs of tiredness — long before crying.

**Awake windows are tiny.** A newborn can only stay comfortably awake for roughly
**45–90 minutes** *including* the feed (some sources say as little as 30–45 min, others
up to ~1.5 hrs — they vary, so trust the cues over the numbers).

Tired cues, in the order they usually appear:

| Stage | What you'll see |
|-------|-----------------|
| **Early (act now)** | Yawning, glazed/staring-into-space look, going quiet, looking away, drooping eyelids, losing interest |
| **Mid** | Rubbing eyes/face, clenching fists, jerky or stiff arm/leg movements, grizzling, becoming over-active |
| **Late (already overtired)** | Fussing and crying — the NHS calls crying a *late* sign; you want to settle before this point |

**Hunger vs. tired?** A hungry baby is usually more *alert* — rooting, turning toward
the breast, sucking sounds, hands to mouth. Rule of thumb: if they fed within the last
~2 hours and are grizzly, they're probably tired. If unsure, offer a feed; if they take
only a little and stay fussy, switch to settling them for sleep.

---

## Step 2 — A simple wind-down (don't overthink it at 1 week)

A one-week-old doesn't need a strict routine yet (that comes around ~3 months). What
helps now is a **short, gentle, repeated sequence** — about 20–30 minutes — that always
happens in the same order, so it slowly becomes a sleep signal:

1. **Calm the environment** — dim the lights, lower your voice, reduce stimulation.
2. **A soothing cue** (optional) — a warm bath or a gentle wipe-down, into clean
   sleepwear.
3. **Feed** — breast or bottle. It's completely fine if they doze off feeding at this age.
4. **Cuddle / skin-to-skin / a soft lullaby** — quiet closeness.
5. **Swaddle** (see Step 3) and place down **on the back** in their own safe sleep space.

> **"Drowsy but awake":** The classic AAP/NHS tip is to lay baby down sleepy but not
> fully asleep, so they learn to drift off on their own. At **one week this is hard and
> totally optional** — newborns often fall asleep at the breast. Don't stress about it
> now; it's a skill that develops over the coming weeks.

## Step 3 — Relaxation techniques that actually work (Harvey Karp's "5 S's")

These mimic the womb and trigger a baby's natural **calming reflex**:

- **Swaddle** — a snug wrap recreates the womb's "hug" and stops the startle reflex
  waking them.
- **Side/Stomach** — holding (never sleeping) the baby on their side or tummy is calming.
- **Shush** — steady white noise / "shhh" mimics the loud whoosh of blood flow they
  heard in utero.
- **Swing** — tiny, gentle jiggling motion (supporting head and neck), then slow rocking
  once calm.
- **Suck** — sucking lowers heart rate and stress (a pacifier or the breast).

**Safety notes on the 5 S's:**

- **Side/stomach is for soothing a *held, awake* baby only — never for sleep.** Babies
  must always be placed **on their back** to sleep.
- **Never shake a baby.** The soothing "jiggle" is *tiny* and fully head-supported — it
  is nothing like shaking, which causes serious brain injury.

**Swaddling safely:**

- Snug around the arms/chest, but **loose around the hips** so legs can bend up and out
  (tight straight-leg wrapping risks hip problems). You should fit 2–3 fingers between
  chest and wrap.
- Use **thin, light fabric** (e.g. muslin) to avoid overheating; don't add blankets on top.
- **Always place a swaddled baby on their back**, and **stop swaddling the moment** baby
  shows any sign of trying to roll (often ~2 months).

**White noise / shushing:** keep it **quiet and at a distance** (a rough rule of thumb is
~50 dB, placed a few feet away — figures vary between sources and aren't a formal
standard) and turn it off once baby is settled.

**Skin-to-skin** calms *both* of you — it steadies baby's heart rate, breathing and
temperature and releases soothing hormones (oxytocin).

**Pacifier note:** A pacifier at sleep is linked to lower SIDS risk — *but* if you're
breastfeeding, the AAP suggests **waiting until feeding is well established (~3–4 weeks)**.
So for a breastfed 1-week-old, it's usually best to hold off for now.

## Step 4 — Gently teach day from night

You can't force a body clock, but you can give helpful cues from birth:

- **During the day:** keep things **bright and normal** — open the curtains, let in
  daylight, talk and interact, allow everyday household noise. Don't tiptoe; don't make
  daytime naps long, dark and silent.
- **At night:** keep it **dark, quiet and boring.** Use only a dim night-light for feeds,
  keep your voice low, **no talking, no play, screens/TV off**, change the diaper only if
  needed, and get baby back down as quickly and calmly as possible.

Consistency matters more than perfection — the same gentle cues, repeated, are what
eventually do the work.

## Step 5 — Safe sleep, every single time (SIDS risk reduction)

This is the non-negotiable part. The core AAP / NHS / Safe to Sleep rules:

- ✅ **Back to sleep** — always on the back, every nap and every night, for the first year.
- ✅ **Firm, flat, non-inclined surface** — a crib, bassinet, or play yard that meets
  safety standards, with a **tightly fitted sheet and nothing else.**
- ✅ **Bare is best** — **no** pillows, loose blankets, bumpers, soft toys, or padding.
- ✅ **Room-share, don't bed-share** — keep baby on their own surface *in your room*,
  ideally for at least the first 6 months (AAP: 6–12 months). This can cut SIDS risk by
  up to ~50%.
- ✅ **Don't overheat** — light layers or a wearable sleep sack; a comfortable room
  (NHS suggests ~16–20 °C). Check the back of the neck — baby shouldn't be sweaty/hot.
- ✅ **Smoke-free** everywhere, always.
- ✅ **Breastfeeding** (if you can) is linked to lower SIDS risk.
- ❌ **Never sleep with baby on a sofa or armchair** — this carries an *extremely* high
  risk (the Lullaby Trust cites up to ~50× higher). Don't let baby routinely sleep in
  car seats, swings, or bouncers either.

**On bed-sharing — sources genuinely disagree, so here's the honest picture:**
- The **AAP** and **Lullaby Trust** advise *against* bed-sharing (adult beds aren't
  designed for infants).
- Because **many** parents bed-share anyway, harm-reduction guidance exists. La Leche
  League's **"Safe Sleep Seven"**: non-smoker; sober/not on sedating meds;
  breastfeeding; healthy full-term baby; baby on back; lightly dressed (not swaddled);
  on a safe firm surface free of pillows, heavy covers and gaps.
- The AAP does **not** endorse any bed-sharing as safe; the Lullaby Trust/NHS take a
  middle harm-reduction line (and say **never** co-sleep if anyone smokes/drinks/takes
  drugs, if baby was premature/low-birth-weight, or on a sofa/armchair). **Discuss your
  situation with your own health professional.**

## Step 6 — Look after *yourself* (a calm parent settles a baby better)

- **Sleep when the baby sleeps.** Let chores wait; rest is the priority.
- **Share the nights.** Trade shifts or alternate feeds/changes with a partner or
  helper. Accept offers of help.
- **Crying spells are normal.** The "**Period of PURPLE Crying**" (~2 weeks to 3–4
  months) is a normal developmental phase of sometimes-inconsolable crying.
- **If you ever feel overwhelmed: put the baby down safely on their back in the crib,
  walk away for 10–15 minutes to breathe and calm down, then come back.** A baby crying
  safely in a crib is fine. **Never shake a baby.**
- **Watch for postpartum/postnatal depression or anxiety** (in either parent): can't
  sleep even when baby sleeps, persistent low mood, hopelessness, trouble coping. Reach
  out to your doctor early.
- **Seek urgent / emergency help immediately** for any thoughts of harming yourself or
  the baby.

## The 60-second version

1. **Don't expect long sleep** — wake-to-feed every 2–3 hrs is normal; you can't spoil them.
2. **Start settling at the first tired cue** (yawn, glazed stare) — not when they cry.
3. **Short calm wind-down:** dim → feed → cuddle → swaddle → down on the back.
4. **Use the 5 S's** to soothe: swaddle, hold on side, shush/white noise, gentle jiggle, suck.
5. **Day = bright & noisy; night = dark, quiet & boring.**
6. **Safe sleep always:** back, firm flat bare surface, own space in your room, no soft bedding.
7. **Rest, share the load, and ask for help** — never shake a baby; if overwhelmed, put
   baby down safely and step away.
